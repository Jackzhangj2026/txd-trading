Cost Comparison: PP Hollow Board vs Wood vs Cardboard

Published: June 7, 2026 | Reading time: 6 minutes

Introduction: Not All Cost Comparisons Are Equal

When international buyers compare packaging materials, the conversation almost always starts with unit price. Cardboard appears cheapest at first glance, wood feels like the premium option for heavy goods, and PP hollow board sits somewhere in between. But this simple price-per-piece comparison misses the most important financial question for any supply chain manager: how many times can I use this package before it needs replacing?

The answer to that question completely transforms the cost picture. A packaging solution that costs three times more upfront but lasts fifty trips can end up being fifty times cheaper per use than a single-use alternative. In this post, we build a practical break-even model that any buyer can apply to their own shipping volumes, trip frequencies, and logistics context — moving beyond generic comparisons to a decision framework tailored to your specific supply chain.

Cost Per Use: The Metric That Matters Most

Cost per use (CPU) is calculated by dividing the total lifetime cost of a packaging unit — including purchase, maintenance, and end-of-life — by the number of trips it completes. This single metric reveals the true economics of packaging choices:

  • Single-use cardboard: CPU equals the unit price plus handling cost. Typical range: $1.50–$4.00 per trip. Cardboard is discarded after one use, so every shipment requires a brand-new box.
  • Plywood crates: Most wooden crates survive 2–5 trips before joints weaken, splinters develop, or ISPM 15 re-treatment is required. CPU range: $8.00–$30.00 per trip for the first year, dropping to $5.00–$12.00 per trip if repaired and re-used.
  • PP hollow board boxes (foldable): High-quality PP boxes with reinforced corners and double-wall construction routinely achieve 30–50+ trips. CPU range: $0.40–$1.20 per trip — often lower than cardboard by the fifth use.

The key insight: even though a PP hollow board box costs 3–5 times more than a cardboard box at point of purchase, its per-trip cost is typically 60–80% lower. The crossover point — where PP becomes cheaper on a cumulative basis — usually arrives between trip 3 and trip 8, depending on box quality and handling conditions.

Break-Even Analysis: When Does PP Hollow Board Pay Off?

The break-even point depends on three variables: upfront unit cost, number of trips per year, and the cost of single-use alternatives. We modelled three common international shipping scenarios:

Scenario A: High-Volume Domestic Distribution (50 trips/year)

A food distributor moves produce crates between a central warehouse and 50 retail locations weekly. Cardboard boxes cost $2.00 each and are single-use. A PP hollow board crate costs $8.00 and survives 50+ trips.

Year 1 cost (cardboard): 50 trips × $2.00 = $100 per box position.
Year 1 cost (PP): $8.00 purchase price + 0 maintenance = $8.00.
Break-even: Trip 4. By trip 4, the buyer has spent $8.00 on cardboard (4 × $2.00) — exactly the cost of one PP crate. Every trip after trip 4 is pure savings with PP. Over 50 trips, total savings per box position: $92.00. For a fleet of 1,000 crates, that is $92,000 in annual savings.

Scenario B: International Ocean Freight (12 trips/year)

An automotive parts exporter ships engine components from Shanghai to Hamburg, 12 times per year. Plywood crates cost $18.00 each and survive 5 trips before needing replacement. A reinforced PP hollow board box costs $12.00 and survives 30+ trips.

Year 1 cost (plywood): 5 crates × $18.00 + 7 more × $18.00 (since the first batch fails after 5 trips) = $216 per box position. Plus ISPM 15 treatment: $5 × 12 = $60. Total: $276.
Year 1 cost (PP): $12.00 × 1 purchase = $12.00. Zero compliance cost.
Break-even: Trip 2. By the second shipment, the buyer has spent $36 on plywood (2 × $18) plus $10 in treatment fees = $46, versus $12 for PP. The PP solution has already paid for itself and saved $34. In year two, the plywood buyer starts over (another $276), while the PP buyer spends $0 — the original box is still in service.

Scenario C: Air Freight for High-Value Electronics (24 trips/year)

A contract manufacturer ships PCB assemblies from Shenzhen to Chicago twice per month. Corrugated cardboard ESD boxes cost $3.50 each. PP anti-static boxes cost $15.00 and survive 40+ trips.

Year 1 cost (cardboard): 24 × $3.50 = $84 per box position. Each cardboard box adds ~800g of weight — at $5/kg air freight, that is an additional $4.00 per trip in pure packaging weight cost. Total: $84 + (24 × $4.00) = $180.
Year 1 cost (PP): $15.00 + 0 weight surcharge (PP is lighter per trip than cardboard for the equivalent protection). Total: $15.00.
Break-even: Trip 4. After trip 4, the buyer has spent $14 on cardboard + $16 in air freight weight = $30, while the PP box cost $15. Every subsequent trip saves $11.50 in avoided cardboard purchases plus $4.00 in freight weight. Over 24 trips: $165 in savings per box position.

Decision Matrix: Which Packaging for Your Situation?

No single material is optimal for every shipping scenario. Use the table below as a quick-reference guide based on your specific operational parameters:

Scenario Recommended Material Rationale
One-off domestic shipment Cardboard Lowest upfront cost, no return logistics needed
Regular domestic loops (10+ trips/year) PP hollow board CPU lower than cardboard by trip 4, zero waste disposal cost
Heavy machinery (500+ kg) Plywood or reinforced PP Evaluate load rating — reinforced PP (8mm+) can match wood at 60% less weight
International ocean freight (regular) PP hollow board No ISPM 15, lower DIM weight, reusable for 30+ trips
Air freight (any volume) PP hollow board Weight savings alone justify the switch within 3–4 trips
Cold chain / perishables PP hollow board Moisture-proof, washable, food-grade options available
Single-use export with no return Cardboard or lightweight PP Lightweight PP adds product protection without much upfront cost

The Hidden Value of Reusability: Beyond the Unit Price

The financial benefits of PP hollow board extend well beyond the direct CPU comparison. Three additional value drivers are frequently overlooked:

Supply chain resilience: Buyers who switch to reusable PP systems build a buffer against cardboard price volatility. Corrugated cardboard prices fluctuated by 35–55% between 2020 and 2025, driven by recycled fiber shortages, energy costs, and demand spikes. PP prices, tied to polymer markets, are comparatively stable and predictable. A reusable system means you buy once and amortize over years — insulating your packaging budget from annual market swings.

Reduced packaging inventory risk: Cardboard buyers typically stock 4–8 weeks of inventory to buffer against supply disruptions. PP hollow board users, with reusable boxes that cycle through the supply chain, maintain a float system where 20–30% more boxes than active trips cover the full loop. Total packaging inventory investment is often lower, even though individual unit cost is higher.

Brand and ESG reporting value: Many multinational buyers now require suppliers to report packaging waste reduction metrics. Switching from single-use cardboard to reusable PP hollow board can reduce packaging waste generation by 90% or more. This creates tangible value in RFQ evaluations, sustainability audits, and corporate ESG scorecards — advantages that don't appear on a unit price list but directly affect contract win rates.

How to Calculate Your Own Break-Even Point

Every supply chain is different. To find your personal break-even point, gather these five numbers:

  1. Current per-trip packaging cost (unit price + assembly labor + disposal fee)
  2. Annual number of trips per box position
  3. PP hollow board upfront cost (quoted by your supplier for your specification)
  4. Expected PP lifespan in trips (ask your supplier for test data — reputable manufacturers provide cycle-test results)
  5. Return logistics cost (if any) for getting empty boxes back to origin

Then divide the PP unit cost by the difference between your current per-trip cost and the PP per-trip cost (PP unit cost ÷ expected lifespan). The result is the number of trips to break-even. If that number is lower than your expected lifespan, the switch delivers positive ROI.

Most of our clients at TXD CO., LTD find break-even between trip 3 and trip 8, with total savings of 50–80% on packaging costs over a three-year horizon. The few cases where PP does not make financial sense involve true one-off shipments with no possibility of return logistics — and even then, lightweight PP can compete with cardboard on price while offering superior product protection.
